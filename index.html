Australia’s public markets run on weekly shopping

Tourists put Australia’s public markets on the map; the people who come back every week keep them open.

Jane Too · October 2026

Attraction or habit?

This page follows four of Australia’s permanent public markets, two in Melbourne and two interstate: Queen Victoria Market, South Melbourne Market, Adelaide Central Market and Sydney Fish Market. Each is both a tourist attraction and a place where people buy their groceries, and the question is which of those two roles keeps it going. The evidence comes in three kinds: Tourism Research Australia’s visitor surveys, the City of Melbourne’s street sensors, and the markets’ own annual reports and plans. The answer matters now: Queen Victoria Market is part-way through a major renewal, and its plan will change the mix of its stalls.

For international visitors, the market outranks the landmark

Ask international visitors where they went in Victoria in 2025, and Queen Victoria Market comes second, behind only the city’s shopping precinct and ahead of the Great Ocean Road. In South Australia, Adelaide Central Market tops the list outright. Sydney Fish Market is the exception: in a state with the Opera House and the Harbour Bridge, it ranks 13th. South Melbourne Market is not among the places the survey asks about, and returns later as the neighbourhood market.

Every state, the same small share

Tourism Research Australia counts a day trip as a deliberate outing that is not part of a daily routine: at least 50 kilometres there and back, and at least four hours from home. Across 2022–24, between 2.4% and 3.2% of these trips in each state included a market. The gaps between states are small enough that a different sample could reorder them. That flatness is the finding: markets are no bigger a draw for day-trippers in one state than in another, and in every state they turn up in roughly the same small share of outings.

What fills the stalls, and what draws the crowd

In 2026, South Melbourne Market published something rare: both how its stalls are divided and why people come. Merchandise and services take 51% of its stalls, groceries 26% and eateries 23%. Yet when visitors name their main reason for coming, 61% come mainly for groceries, 32% to eat, and only 6% for merchandise. A quarter of the stalls brings in most of the crowd.

The market held its ground while the city emptied

When Melbourne locked down in March 2020, its city centre emptied, but the streets around Queen Victoria Market held on to more of their crowd: across March to June they kept 50.9% of their 2019 foot traffic, against 33.2% for the rest of the city centre. The two fell almost in step in March, but by May the market’s streets had fallen 28 points less than the city centre. South Melbourne Market, counted at its doors, was down just 12% that month. The recovery was slower than the fall: by late 2022, when the sensor record ends, the market’s streets had refilled to only two-thirds of 2019. Even Saturdays, the busiest day, ran about a quarter below 2019.

The locals’ market came back first

South Melbourne Market is a neighbourhood market: two in three of its shoppers live locally, and the average shopper comes back about 42 times a year. Its annual counts show the recovery from inside the gates. By 2023-24, the market was counting 7% more entries than in the 2019-20 financial year. On the other hand, Queen Victoria Market, where three in ten visits before the pandemic were made by tourists, was still 119,927 visitors short. Each market is compared only with its own past: South Melbourne Market counts entries and Queen Victoria Market counts Day Market visitors.

Nearly half of all visitors go to a market

Tourists still matter: in 2025, 46.1% of Australia’s 8.3 million international visitors went to a market. They came from dozens of countries, and no single country supplied more than an eighth of that crowd. But the appetite varied widely. From Taiwan, two in three visitors went to a market (67.7%). Fewer than one in three New Zealanders did (30.0%), the lowest rate in every survey year since 2011, even though New Zealand sends Australia more visitors than any other country.

The crowd came back on the same timetable

The pandemic changed the size of the crowd around Queen Victoria Market, but not its timetable. In 2019 and again from October 2025 to October 2026, Saturday was the busiest day at the Therry Street sensor and Monday the quietest; the market is closed on Mondays. Between the two periods, no day’s share of the week’s traffic moved by as much as two points. The hours held too: on six days out of seven, the busiest hour was at lunchtime both times. The exception in both periods is Wednesday, which peaked at 7pm: the day market is closed on Wednesdays, and in summer and winter the market opens in the evening for its night markets instead. In 2026, the fifteen Wednesdays of the Winter Night Market drew about twice as many people at 7pm as at noon. Six years and a pandemic later, the weekly shoppers keep the same hours.

What renewal has to protect

Queen Victoria Market is being renewed, and on paper its plan reads the crowd well. It grows fresh produce, which its own strategy calls “the Market’s biggest draw for Melburnians”, from 24% to 27% of the mix. It also shrinks specialty shopping from 68% to 55%: the trade that at South Melbourne Market fills half the stalls but is the main reason for only 6 visitors in 100.

Its boldest move is on eateries and food stalls, which more than double from 8% to 18%. South Melbourne Market suggests the appetite is real: nearly a third of its visitors now come mainly to eat, up from a fifth a decade ago. Even so, groceries are the main draw for nearly twice as many.

Every trend this page tracks over time tells the same story. In lockdown, the streets around Queen Victoria Market held on to more of their crowd than the rest of the city centre. South Melbourne Market, the neighbourhood market where two in three shoppers live locally, recovered first. Six years on, the crowd still arrives at lunchtime on the same days.

The test of renewal is simple: does the weekly shopping survive it? Tables may draw new visitors, but the data keeps pointing to groceries as what brings people back. Keep the grocers, and the tables are a bonus. Lose them, and the market becomes one more place to eat in a city that has plenty.
